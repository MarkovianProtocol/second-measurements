SECOND MEASUREMENT SM-019

A federal grant sub-award must be reported by the end of the month after it is made. GAO found 41% of non-pandemic sub-awards late through 2023; on every sub-award at or above the reporting threshold made since October 2017 it is {{LATEPCT}}, and state agencies carry three-quarters of the late reports

Markovian ProtocolMeasured 2026-10-12Status: published 2026-10-13; questions drafted, not yet sentDOI: {{DOI}}

When a federal grant is passed down to a sub-recipient, a school district, a county, a university lab, the prime recipient must report the sub-award to the government by the end of the month after it was made; the rule is an award term in federal grants, 2 CFR 170, Appendix A, and covers every sub-award of $30,000 or more, $25,000 before 12 November 2020. The reports are public on USAspending.gov with two dates on each: the day the sub-award was made and the month the report was published. Until March 2025 recipients filed through FSRS, the Federal Funding Accountability and Transparency Act Subaward Reporting System; since then the same reports go through SAM.gov, the government’s award-registration site. GAO read the file in 2023 and found 2.4 million of nearly 6 million records late. We recomputed it on the file as it stands on 12 October 2026, every grant sub-award made since October 2017 at or above the threshold, {{RECORDS}} of them once duplicate reports are removed and the {{BELOW}} below the threshold, which carry no deadline, are set aside: {{LATE}} were reported after the deadline ({{LATEPCT}}), {{LATE3}} three or more months after. The late ones sum to {{LATEUSD}}, after setting aside {{EXCLN}} whose amount is larger than its own prime award, a category GAO itself flagged as likely reporting error (the raw sum, those included, is {{LATEUSDRAW}}). Every fiscal year here reads lower than it will finish, because a sub-award not yet reported is not in the file; FY2024, the most mature year before reporting moved into SAM.gov, stands at {{FY24P}} today, and stood at {{FY24AT13}} at the age FY2025 is now. Prime recipients registered as state governments carry {{SGL}} of the {{LATE}} late reports ({{SGP}}), most of them agencies passing federal education and nutrition money to districts and counties; {{TOPNAME}}, the name as filed, reported {{TOPL}} of its {{TOPR}} sub-awards late. GAO’s file, read on 1 August 2023, could not contain any report filed after that day: of the {{GWN}} sub-awards in our set made before August 2023, {{GWAFTER}} were reported in August 2023 or later, so GAO’s count was a floor; on the reports GAO could see, our test gives {{GWSEEN}} late.

The short version
  • 2 CFR 170, Appendix A, the award term in federal grants: a recipient “must report each subaward that equals or exceeds $30,000 in Federal funds” and, “for subaward information, report no later than the end of the month following the month in which the subaward was issued.”1 The threshold was $25,000 before 12 November 2020.8 The example in the rule: made on 7 November, due by 31 December.
  • USAspending.gov publishes every reported grant sub-award with the sub-award’s action date, the day it was made, and the year and month the prime recipient’s report was published. We pulled the whole set for sub-awards made 1 October 2017 to 12 October 2026 through the site’s bulk-download service, {{ROWS}} rows. GAO found about a quarter of records are duplicates, so one sub-award here is one prime award, sub-award number and action date, and where it was reported more than once the earliest report counts: {{DUPS}} later reports of the same sub-award dropped ({{DUPAMT}} of them carrying a different amount, so many are amendments rather than copies), {{BEFORE}} reports whose month is earlier than the sub-award’s month dropped, {{BELOW}} sub-awards whose earliest reported amount is below the threshold set aside (the rule sets no deadline for them), {{RECORDS}} sub-awards scored.
  • A sub-award is late when its report month is after the month following the action month, the rule’s own resolution. {{LATE}} of {{RECORDS}} ({{LATEPCT}}) were late; {{LATE3}} by three months or more; {{LATE12}} by a year or more; the median late report came {{MEDLATE}} months after the deadline month. The late-reported sub-awards sum to {{LATEUSD}}; {{EXCLN}} of them, {{LATEUSDEXCL}}, have an amount larger than their own prime award and are set aside from that sum, GAO’s own “impossibly high” and “greater than prime award” categories (the raw sum, those included, is {{LATEUSDRAW}}).
  • GAO’s figure, October 2010 to August 2023: 41% of non-pandemic grant sub-awards and 30% of pandemic-funded ones reported late, scored record by record, duplicates included.2 Ours, on sub-awards made since October 2017, one report counted per sub-award: non-pandemic {{NCL}} of {{NCR}} ({{NCP}}), pandemic-funded {{COVL}} of {{COVR}} ({{COVP}}), the opposite order.7 Part of the gap is time: {{GWAFTER}} of the {{GWN}} sub-awards made before August 2023 were reported after GAO’s read, and a report that has not been filed is absent from everyone’s count. On the reports GAO could have seen, our one-report-per-sub-award test gives {{GWSEEN}} late overall, {{GWNC}} non-pandemic and {{GWCOV}} pandemic-funded. Scored GAO’s own way on that same window, every report and every duplicate counted, the figure is {{GWRECORDS}} ({{GWRECTHR}} at or above the threshold), above GAO’s 41%/30% rather than below it, so duplicates do not explain the gap; what is left is window (GAO counted from October 2010, we from October 2017) and clock (GAO’s submission date from inside the reporting system, our publication month from the public file), and we put the clock question to GAO below. FY2018: {{FY18P}}. FY2021 and FY2022, the pandemic years: {{FY21P}} and {{FY22P}}. FY2025: {{FY25P}}, about where a year sits at this age before it rises further. FY2026 so far: {{FY26L}} of {{FY26R}} ({{FY26P}}), with its late reports still to come.
  • By prime recipient, the organisation that owes the report: {{NPR}} recipients have 200 or more sub-awards. {{TOPNAME}}: {{TOPL}} of {{TOPR}} late ({{TOPP}}). The table below names the twenty-five with the most late reports, all of them state departments or agencies, and eight large recipients with the lowest late share.

Details

The file carries two date fields for the report. Its month and year, which the data dictionary defines as the month “in which a given report … was published by the prime awardee”,3 and its last-modified date, which the dictionary maps to the system’s date-submitted field. On all {{RECORDS}} scored sub-awards the last-modified date falls inside the report month and the late verdict is the same under either, so the month is the submission month and we use it, the rule’s own resolution; a sub-award made on any day of a month and reported in the next calendar month is on time, reported the month after that is one month late. Because a report that has not been filed is not in the file at all, every fiscal year here reads lower than it will finish; the fiscal-year table below shows how much, comparing each year’s current share with the share it carried at the age FY2025 is now. GAO also found that a quarter of records are likely duplicates, the same sub-award reported twice. We count one sub-award as one prime award, sub-award number and action date, and when it appears under more than one report we keep the earliest, which is the first time the government heard of it and favours the recipient; scored GAO’s own way instead, every report counted, our window only climbs further above GAO’s 41%/30%, so duplicates are not why it sits there. The pandemic split follows the pandemic disaster emergency fund codes USAspending itself documents, L, M, N, O, P and U (the CARES Act through the December 2020 relief law) and V (the American Rescue Plan Act), for a sub-award made from April 2020 at month resolution. GAO’s own definition of the term, footnote 15, says a sub-award made “on or after April 1, 2020” (inclusive, matching our choice, which keeps the {{APR1}} pandemic-funded sub-awards made on 1 April itself) and names no codes at all, only “a prime award that was given a DEFC to indicate that it received pandemic funding”; our seven-code list is at least as inclusive as that.2 A different passage elsewhere in the same report says “after April 1, 2020” instead, so GAO’s own report does not agree with itself on the date; and the “L” through “O” list some readers may know from this report is from a different footnote, used only to help GAO pick which three agencies to interview, not to define the term behind figure 5.7 Dollar totals exclude a late sub-award whose amount is larger than its own prime award, GAO’s own “greater than prime award” and “impossibly high” categories, which OMB (the Office of Management and Budget) told GAO should not happen and are likely reporting errors, though GSA says a few are legitimate when a sub-award is deliberately structured larger than the prime.2 Recipients also report sub-awards below the $30,000 threshold ($25,000 before 12 November 2020): {{BELOW}} of the sub-awards in the file have an earliest reported amount under it. The rule sets no deadline for those, so they are set aside and not scored; the test uses the amount on the earliest report, so the {{BELOWRAISED}} sub-awards first reported small and later raised to the threshold or above are set aside too, though a modification that reaches the threshold is reportable. Set-aside sub-awards are not in the rows file; their count is below_threshold in the summary json.8 A recipient whose own gross income was under $300,000 in the prior tax year is exempt from reporting any of its sub-awards at all, a separate, recipient-level exemption.1

How this ends

One act is already available without anyone’s help: a prime recipient files the report in the month after the sub-award, and the next monthly read would see its late share for new sub-awards fall toward zero. The other acts are GAO’s own four recommendations, none confirmed done as of this read. GSA was told to add automated validations for known data-quality limits such as wrong amounts (Recommendation 1) and to give recipients guidance against duplicative reporting (Recommendation 2); it now runs the reporting system inside SAM.gov, and our question to GSA above asks whether either has been built in. Treasury was told to disclose subaward data-quality limitations on the USAspending.gov pages where users see the data (Recommendation 3); this page is one more such disclosure. And OMB was told to clarify what agencies must do about a recipient that reports late (Recommendation 4) — GAO found, and the agency officials it interviewed confirmed, that current OMB guidance does not hold agencies responsible for subaward data quality and is unclear on what, if anything, they owe a non-compliant recipient. We re-pull the newest two fiscal years on the first of each month, re-score every sub-award in them, and print the result on the rechecks page by fiscal year and for the recipients named here.

Exhibit 1 · The rule

“(1) Applicability. Unless the recipient is exempt as provided in paragraph (d) of this award term, the recipient must report each subaward that equals or exceeds $30,000 in Federal funds … (2) Reporting Requirements. What we can’t be sure of

  • The file is the recipients’ own reports. A sub-award never reported is not in it, so every count here is a floor on what is owed and late; the newest months read best for that reason.
  • Every fiscal year here can still rise as late reports arrive, as the fiscal-year table above shows; at the age FY2025 is now, FY2019 read {{C19A}} and has since risen to {{C19B}}, FY2021 read {{C21A}} and rose to {{C21B}}. The full year-by-year comparison is censoring_at_13_months in the summary json.
  • The report month is the file’s publication month and the last-modified date, mapped by the dictionary to the system’s date-submitted field, never falls outside it on any of the {{RECORDS}} records (clock_check in the summary). GAO used a submission date from inside the system; if that date can differ from the published month for reports created in one month and published in the next, GAO’s clock and ours differ by up to a month on those.
  • One sub-award is one prime award, sub-award number and action date. A recipient that reports the same sub-award under two numbers is counted twice; one that corrects a date creates what we read as a new sub-award. GAO’s duplicate estimate was a quarter of records; ours removed {{DUPS}} later reports of the same sub-award. Scored GAO’s way instead, every report counted, the late share only climbs, so this is not where our figure diverges from GAO’s. {{REPEATS}} late sub-awards share a recipient, sub-award number and action month with a report filed in an earlier month; {{REPOTHER}} of them sit under a different prime award, which owes its own report. {{REREPORT}} match an earlier report under another prime award on sub-recipient, amount and day, the likely re-reports; counting them as duplicates would move the headline by {{REREPORTPTS}}.
  • Dollar totals exclude {{EXCLN}} late sub-awards whose amount is larger than their own prime award, {{LATEUSDEXCL}} set aside from the {{LATEUSDRAW}} raw sum; GAO found 25,260 such records across the whole file and quoted OMB calling the pattern likely reporting error, but GSA told GAO a few are legitimate, so this is a conservative filter, not a certainty every excluded record is wrong.
  • Recipients also report sub-awards below the $30,000 threshold ($25,000 before 12 November 2020); the {{BELOW}} whose earliest reported amount is under it are set aside, and a recipient that judged a sub-award below it and never reported it is not in the file at all. A recipient whose own gross income was under $300,000 in the prior tax year is exempt from reporting any of its sub-awards at all, a separate, recipient-level exemption.
  • The action date is typed by the filer. Reports whose month is earlier than the sub-award’s month, {{BEFORE}}, are dropped; a wrong action date inside the plausible range would move a record between on time and late and we cannot see it.
  • Pandemic funding is read from the prime award’s disaster emergency fund codes (L, M, N, O, P, U, V); a sub-award under a prime that carried both pandemic and regular money counts as pandemic-funded.
  • “State governments carry three-quarters of the late reports” is read from a self-reported business-type field, and the field has at least one known miss among the top 25 recipients: California’s education department is a state agency by name but not self-tagged “State Government”. Fixing that one case alone would push the share higher. {{SGP}} is a floor, not the exact number.
  • One snapshot, 12 October 2026, from one source, USAspending’s bulk download. The same request on another day returns a different file; the hashes of ours are in the exhibits.

Run it yourself

The script uses only Python’s standard library and reads the zips without unpacking them. USAspending builds each file on request, one fiscal year at a time.

curl -s -X POST https://api.usaspending.gov/api/v2/bulk_download/awards/ -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{"filters":{"sub_award_types":["grant"],"date_type":"action_date","date_range":{"start_date":"2024-10-01","end_date":"2025-09-30"},"agencies":[{"type":"awarding","tier":"toptier","name":"All"}]},"file_format":"csv"}'
# repeat per fiscal year; poll the status_url until "finished", fetch file_url
curl -O https://markovianprotocol.com/measurements/sm-019/subaward_lag.py
python3 -I subaward_lag.py out/ All_Subawards_*.zip       # prints the summary; writes out/subaward_lag_summary.json and out/subaward_lag_rows.csv

Ran it? Send the number you got and the date to hello@markovianprotocol.com, subject “Rerun SM-019”. We print it on the track record with your name, or just the date if you prefer, and the first outside rerun of each paper gets the same seal a recipient gets for answering or fixing.

What you can do with this

  • If you run grants compliance for a state agency or a university with 200 or more sub-awards since 2018, your office’s row is in subaward_lag_summary.json under your UEI; every scored sub-award is in the rows file under your UEI whatever the count ({{NOUEI}} rows carry no prime UEI). Your table row gives the median months your office’s late reports run.
  • If you are a sub-recipient, a district or a county, the rows file carries your name, your UEI and the sub-award number, so you can see when the money you received was reported.
  • If you write about federal spending data, every number above comes from one public download and one script; rerun it on this month’s file before quoting it.
